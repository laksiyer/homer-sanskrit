# Iliad 1.43–60 | Apollo’s arrows and the assembly

> [!warning] Working edition
> Sanskrit prose and Roman Greek transcription are provisional. The Greek remains authoritative. The English below is an original close reading aid, not a quotation from Murray. Line 60 ends in the middle of Achilles’ sentence, which continues in 1.61.

## Sanskrit, Devanāgarī

एवं प्रार्थयमानस्य तस्य वचनम् अपोल्लोनः फोइबः अशृणोत्। स हृदि क्रुद्धः ओलुम्पपर्वतस्य शिखरेभ्यः अवतीर्णः। तस्य स्कन्धयोर्धनुः उभयतः संवृतश्चेषुधिः आस्ताम्। स क्रुद्धो यदा प्राचलत् तदा तस्य स्कन्धयोः शरा झणझणायन्त स्म। स नक्तमिव आगच्छत्।

अथ स नौभ्यो दूरे उपविश्य शरम् असृजत्। तस्य रजतधनुषो घोरः शब्दोऽभवत्। प्रथमं स अश्वतरान् शीघ्रगामिनश्च शुनः अविध्यत्। अनन्तरं मनुष्यान् एव तीक्ष्णैः शरैरविध्यत्। मृतानां चिताश्च निरन्तरं बह्व्यः प्रजज्वलुः।

नवाहानि देवस्य शराः सेनामध्ये विचेरुः। दशमेऽहनि अखिल्लेउसः जनान् सभायाम् आह्वयत्। श्वेतबाहुः हेरा देवी हि तस्य मनसि एतद् अधात्। सा हि म्रियमाणान् दानाओइजनान् दृष्ट्वा तेषु अनुकम्पाम् अकरोत्।

अथ सर्वेषु समागतेषु शीघ्रपादः अखिल्लेउसः उत्थाय अवदत्, “हे अत्रेयपुत्र, यदि वयं मृत्योर्मुच्येमहि, तर्हि पुनः प्रत्यावर्त्य स्वगृहान् गमिष्याम इति मन्ये।”

## Comparative notes

**1.47: νυκτί / नक्त्.** Greek *nyktí* (dative of 'night') and Vedic *nákt-* belong to an inherited Indo-European night word family. The running Sanskrit **नक्तमिव** draws on the Vedic form, preserving the image of Apollo approaching like night. Compare Vedic *naktam* ('by night').

**1.50: οὐρῆας / अश्वतरान्.** Homer refers to *mules* (οὐρῆας), not ordinary donkeys. The close Sanskrit term is **अश्वतरान्**. There is no cognate assertion for the Greek animal name here.

**1.50: κύνας / शुनः.** Greek *kýōn* (stem *kyn-*) and Sanskrit *śvan-* 'dog' are established Indo-European cognates. Our prose uses **शुनः**.

**1.53: ἐννῆμαρ / नवाहानि.** The numerical base Greek *ennéa* and Sanskrit *náva* 'nine' are established cognates. The entire compounds are not identical inherited words.

**1.54: δεκάτῃ / दशमे.** Greek *déka* and Sanskrit *dáśa* 'ten' are established cognates. The prose uses the ordinal construction **दशमेऽहनि**.

**1.55: Ἥρη / हेरा.** Hera is retained as a Greek divine name. **Śacī/Indrāṇī (शची/इन्द्राणी)** provides a limited comparison in her role as the spouse of the chief god; this is a *functional analogy*, not a secure cognate, and her intervention for the dying Achaeans is specifically Homeric. Compare the Greek text linked below. The original is **λευκώλενος Ἥρη**, “white-armed Hera”; **श्वेतबाहुः हेरा** preserves the epithet without identifying her with Indrāṇī.

**1.58: πόδας ὠκύς / शीघ्रपादः.** 'Swift-footed' is a faithful semantic rendering of the recurring Homeric epithet, not an established lexical cognate pairing.

## English reading aid (collapsible)

> [!note]- English translation (show when needed)
> So he prayed, and Phoebus Apollo heard him. Angered in his heart, the god came down from the heights of Olympus with his bow and a covered quiver upon his shoulders. As he moved, the arrows rattled against his shoulders; he came like night. He sat away from the ships and sent an arrow forth, and a terrible sound arose from the silver bow. First he attacked the mules and swift dogs; then he struck the men with sharp arrows. Funeral pyres for the dead burned continually. For nine days the god’s missiles passed through the camp. On the tenth Achilles called the people into assembly. White-armed Hera had put this into his mind, for she pitied the Danaans when she saw them dying. When they had gathered, swift-footed Achilles rose and said to the son of Atreus: “I think we may now have to turn back and return home, if indeed we can escape death.”
